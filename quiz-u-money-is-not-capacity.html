A Capital Bond for a Payroll Problem?

Berkeley's General Fund has run a structural deficit for years, driven in part by rising personnel costs. In March, the City Manager told Council that Public Works was shifting staff off the General Fund and that bond dollars could "offset some of those staff challenges." Berkeley's $300 million infrastructure bond cites the General Fund deficit, and the City's plan for it sets aside $40.5 million for staffing. How much of that buys new capacity?

Question 1 of 6

At the March 17, 2026 special meeting, Councilmember Shoshana O'Keefe asked how the proposed measures would help Berkeley's structural deficit. What did staff identify as one benefit of the bond?

The bond does not create unrestricted General Fund revenue. Measure U's proceeds may be used "only for the purposes specified in this Measure." Staff's answer pointed to personnel costs instead:

"That's not so much focused on, like, a budget reduction, although, of course, we would be hiring engineers and a lot of staff to do the work, because it is a lot of work, and part of our budget deficit challenge in public works includes shifting a lot of people off of general fund and into special funds. And so… if we add more special fund dollars through this measure to address the infrastructure needs that we have, we would be able to, you know, offset some of those staff challenges, I would say."

That is the City's stated plan: Public Works is moving staff costs off the General Fund into special funds, and the bond's special-fund dollars would help carry them.

That makes the General Fund look better. It does not tell us whether Berkeley became cheaper to operate, or whether the work charged to the bond is new. The burden is on the City to show which.

The question is what changed: the work, or the account paying for it?

O'Keefe is identified from the Chair's call immediately before her question. The answer is attributed to the City Manager by the staff member who added to it: "as the city manager said." Measure U, §6(B).

Question 2 of 6

How much did Berkeley's May 19 working framework set aside for staffing and implementation in the proposed bond program?

The working framework showed:

  • $300 million in bond sales;
  • about $13 million in assumed interest earnings;
  • $272.5 million for projects; and
  • $40.5 million for staffing and implementation.

That $40.5 million is large enough to deserve its own explanation.

Voters should know how much buys new capacity and how much replaces costs Berkeley already pays.

Source: May 19, 2026 Special Item 01 staff report, "Current Working Financial Framework for the GO Bond," p. 16. The project list in the same report carries the line as project manager costs (salary, workstations, software, auditing, supplies) "over 12 years" (p. 63). The June 16, 2026 report that placed Measure U on the ballot repeats the figures (Item 38, p. 2).

In fairnessThe $40.5 million is a working-framework figure, not a term of the measure. Measure U sets no staffing amount; it says each improvement includes its share of "planning, program management and construction costs." The City's report says these costs "are intended to support bond program delivery and administration, not ongoing operations or routine maintenance."

Question 3 of 6

Suppose Measure U fails. An employee Berkeley had planned to charge partly to the bond stays employed, stays on the General Fund, and keeps doing useful work. What does that tell us?

It tells us the employee has useful work that does not depend on Measure U.

The City Manager has said the bond will help move Public Works staff costs off the General Fund. The open question is what work those charges pay for. An existing engineer can legitimately move time to newly funded capital work.

Where the work charged to Measure U is work Berkeley would have performed anyway, paid from the General Fund, Measure U is carrying an existing General Fund cost. That frees General Fund money without adding delivery capacity.

The staffing bridge in the last question is how the City can show which it is.

Question 4 of 6

An existing employee is already fully occupied. Measure U gives that employee substantial new bond work. What has to happen?

People have finite time.

If Measure U work is truly new, it uses capacity that was previously doing something else.

Berkeley should be able to show what happens to that old work.

If it cannot, "new bond capacity" may simply mean that the same organization is being billed differently.

Question 5 of 6

Berkeley's earlier T1 infrastructure bond authorized $100 million. Measure U would authorize $300 million, three times as much. What should Berkeley have shown before deciding how large a new bond to seek?

More money does not automatically produce proportionally more completed work. The three-to-one ratio describes the borrowing, not the workload: both bonds are issued over time, so how much more the City must run at once depends on timing and staffing.

A much larger program can require:

  • more project managers;
  • more supervisors;
  • more office space and equipment;
  • more procurement staff;
  • more inspectors;
  • more consultants;
  • more coordination among teams;
  • more contract management; and
  • more control over who is hiring the same contractors for overlapping work.

Berkeley's own planning named that work early and left it open as the dollar figure firmed up. On December 2, 2025, the Mayor's Vision 2050 Task Force report before Council called on the City to "Develop an organization structure and staffing needed to deliver the projects," and staff's bond report listed "staff capacity" among the factors still to be analyzed. On March 17, staff said it was "considering the staffing organization and the staffing architecture that needs to be in place to deliver a bond of this magnitude." On May 19, asked how the City would staff and organize the work, the City Manager answered: "we haven't come up with exactly what the staffing model would look like."

Council's discussion of the amount weighed polling, tax rates and the size of the backlog.

A $300 million authorization needs a delivery-capacity case, not just a polling case.

Source: Measure T1 authorized $100,000,000 and was issued in three series (June 30, 2026 Item 44, pp. 1, 3); Measure U is planned in $100 million installments every five years from 2027 (May 19, 2026 Special Item 01, p. 4). Realize Vision 2050, December 2, 2025 Special Item 01, pp. 11, 24. December 2, 2025 Special Item 02 staff report, p. 10. City captions, March 17, 2026, 00:30:25 (p. 22). Question 6 of 6

Which evidence would best show that Measure U's staffing money is buying additional capacity rather than protecting the General Fund from existing payroll costs?

This is the test that settles the question. The City should be able to draw a simple bridge:

Before Measure U

  • these people;
  • doing this work;
  • paid from these funds.

With Measure U

  • this new work;
  • these added or reassigned people;
  • this old work reassigned, reduced, delayed, or contracted out;
  • this additional management and support;
  • these Measure U charges.

If Measure U fails

  • this is what happens instead.

Without that bridge, voters cannot tell how much of the $40.5 million adds capacity and how much simply moves existing costs.

Quiz complete

Now you know

Berkeley assigned $40.5 million to staffing before it had a staffing model showing what existing staff would shift, what work would be backfilled, what positions would be new, and how much capacity the bond would add.

On March 17, the City Manager said Public Works' deficit challenge included shifting people off the General Fund, and that bond dollars could "offset some of those staff challenges." Staff said it was still considering "the staffing architecture." On May 19, Councilmember Blackaby asked how the City would staff and organize the work. The City Manager answered that the plan had "built in the additional costs to add staff" but "we haven't come up with exactly what the staffing model would look like."

Neither the May 19 framework nor the June 16 report that placed Measure U on the ballot shows how much of the $40.5 million adds capacity and how much carries work Berkeley already pays for.

Money is not capacity.
