A Capital Bond for a Payroll Problem?

Money Is Not Capacity: Measure U’s $40 Million Staffing Line

Berkeley’s working plan for Measure U sets aside $40.5 million for staffing and implementation. The City developed the allowance while confronting a persistent General Fund deficit and before it had decided what the staffing model would be. Voters haven’t been shown how much of that $40.5 million buys new capacity, and how much just moves payroll off a General Fund that can’t pay for it.

The rule is simple

PracticeWhy
OKAn employee spends 30% of their time on Measure U projects; 30% of their cost is charged to U.The bond pays for the work actually performed for the bond program.
OKAn existing employee moves substantially onto U work, and the City documents who takes over their previous work.The staffing plan shows where the additional capacity comes from.
NOT OKAn employee is charged 100% to U while continuing to spend substantial time on unrelated City work.The bond pays for work it did not receive, while the underlying General Fund workload remains.
NOT ENOUGHThe City budgets $40.5M for “staffing and implementation” without showing staff assignments and time allocation.The auditor—and therefore voters—cannot determine what work the bond actually paid for or how much additional capacity it bought.

Why this deserves scrutiny

Berkeley’s proposed FY 2027–2028 budget says the City has faced a “persistent General Fund structural deficit for many years” and identifies rising personnel costs as one cause. Measure U itself recites projected General Fund deficits of $31 million in FY 2027 and $29 million in FY 2028.

On March 17, Councilmember Shoshana O’Keefe asked how the proposed ballot measures would address the structural deficit. The City Manager’s answer connected the infrastructure bond directly to Public Works staffing:

“[P]art of our budget deficit challenge in public works includes shifting a lot of people off of general fund and into special funds. And so… if we add more special fund dollars through this measure… we would be able to… offset some of those staff challenges.”

City Manager, March 17, 2026 special meeting, 00:44:27–00:44:59 (City captions)

That puts the burden on the City: before voters approve $40.5 million for staffing, it should show that the money buys new delivery capacity rather than carrying the payroll the General Fund can no longer afford.

Two months later, the working framework assigned $40.5 million to staffing and implementation. Councilmember Brent Blackaby asked how the City would staff and organize the program. The City Manager answered:

“[W]e haven’t come up with exactly what the staffing model would look like, but we’ve built in the additional costs to add staff to run the measure…. We haven’t just made that decision yet….”

City Manager, May 19, 2026 special meeting, 01:31:12–01:31:45 (City captions)
The $40.5 million was in the framework. The staffing model it would pay for was not.

Moving existing payroll onto a temporary bond can make the General Fund deficit smaller on paper without rightsizing the underlying organization or eliminating the recurring cost. When the bond ends, the fiscal problem remains.

So what does the $40.5 million actually buy: additional capacity to deliver Measure U, or relief for a General Fund the City’s own budget says has run a structural deficit for years?

Show the staffing bridge

The City can answer that question with a compact, auditable staffing bridge:

ShowWhat voters should be able to see
BaselineEach employee’s existing work and current funding source
Measure U workThe new bond work assigned to that employee and the percentage of time charged to U
Old workWhat stops, slows, is reassigned, contracted out, or backfilled
New capacityNew hires, added hours, consultants, management, and support attributable to U
No-bond caseWhat happens to the positions and workload if U fails

Those records should allow the auditor to trace payroll charges to actual Measure U work and allow voters to see how much additional delivery capacity their borrowing purchased.

The standard

An infrastructure bond is a commitment of capital to deliver durable projects. Using borrowed money to carry existing payroll—and avoid hard decisions about the size and work of the organization—would be an abuse of taxpayer trust.
