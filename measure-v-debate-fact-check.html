Fact-Checking Berkeley’s Measure V Debate

Mayor Adena Ishii and Eric Friedman debated Berkeley’s proposed half-cent sales tax. We checked the consequential factual claims and the reasoning used to support them.

Berkeley Decision Project · Wellstone Democratic Renewal Club debate, moderated by Soli Alpert · Published September 3, 2026
Disclosure

This site is published by Eric Friedman, who argued against Measure V in this debate. He is one of the two people being fact-checked here.

AI disclosure: AI tools assisted substantially with analysis and drafting. Eric Friedman selected the claims, supplied and reviewed the evidence, edited the analysis, and takes responsibility for the conclusions.

Rather than reproduce the entire debate, this article isolates the statements that carry factual or logical weight. The quotations remain in chronological order so readers can follow the argument. We check statements by both speakers. Policy judgments are not rated true or false merely because we agree or disagree with them.

Quotations are lightly edited from an automated transcript of the debate audio, correcting obvious transcription errors and punctuation. Substantive wording is preserved, and ellipses mark omitted material within a quote.

How to read the ratings

Supported
The available record directly supports the material claim.
Needs context
Substantially grounded, but missing context changes its significance.
Misleading
Contains a factual basis but materially distorts scale, scope, chronology or category.
Incorrect
Contradicted by the available evidence or by the relevant definition.
Unsupported
The reviewed public record does not establish the claim. This is not the same as false.
Analysis
A prediction, inference or normative judgment rather than a directly checkable fact.

Reasoning notes are separate from factual ratings. A statement can be factually true and still fail to support the conclusion it is offered for.

Adena Ishii00:52
“The City of Berkeley has been struggling with a budget deficit. We just closed a $30 million deficit in this last budget cycle. As you can imagine, the federal government has not been very kind to us recently. Between the federal government and the state government, we have unfortunately lost a lot of funding that normally comes down to the local level.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Needs context

The chronology matters. Berkeley’s structural General Fund imbalance predates the recent federal and state funding pressures. The City Auditor described the structural deficit as a multi-year problem; the City Manager described it as persistent for many years; and Ishii herself had called it a “longstanding structural deficit.” Recent outside funding losses can worsen Berkeley’s finances, but they do not explain the origin of the roughly $30 million structural gap.

ReasoningPlacing the two statements back-to-back encourages a causal inference the chronology does not support. The newer funding pressure and the older structural imbalance should be analyzed separately.
Sources: City Auditor, Berkeley’s Financial Condition (FY2016–FY2025); FY2027–FY2028 budget adoption record; Mayor’s April 2026 budget message; 2026 General Obligation Bonds Official Statement. See in transcript →
Adena Ishii01:21
“And what is the sales tax for, you may ask? It’s for very essential public safety needs. So that includes police, fire, and also youth programming.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Incorrect

Measure V is a general tax, not a public-safety tax. The City Attorney’s impartial analysis states its revenue “would be placed into the General Fund and could be spent on any valid municipal purpose.” The measure does not earmark revenue for police, fire, youth programming, or public safety generally, and it guarantees no particular staffing or service level.

The word doing the work is essential — and it is a word about rank. So are “core needs,” which Ishii uses later in the debate, and “nothing is more important than the safety of our families,” which opens the argument she signed. All three describe some things mattering more than others. Nothing in the record performs that sorting: the ordinance ranks nothing, a general tax funds whatever a future Council decides, and the FY2027–28 budget was built by across-the-board departmental reductions, a method that declines to rank by design. What is offered is the language of priority without the act of setting one.

ReasoningIshii then argues that Berkeley should consider youth programming part of public safety. That may be a legitimate policy preference, but it is not a restriction contained in Measure V. Her own explanation illustrates the discretion built into a general tax: after voters approve the revenue, elected officials still decide which programs are “essential,” what counts as “public safety,” and what gets protected. The accountability issue is not whether youth programming has value; it is that the measure does not bind the City to the purpose Ishii describes.
Sources: City Attorney’s Impartial Analysis of Measure V; Measure V ordinance, June 16, 2026; Argument in Favor of Measure V. See in transcript →
Adena Ishii01:39
“I know a lot of folks say, hey, sales tax, that’s regressive. I have an issue with that. And to that, I would say … this half-cent increase would actually only just put us on par with Oakland, Hayward, Albany, and other cities within Alameda County.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Misleading

Ishii names the objection and then answers a different question. Regressivity is about who bears the burden — a sales tax takes a larger share of income from lower-income households, because they spend more of what they earn. What Oakland and Hayward charge has no bearing on that. A tax levied everywhere is not less regressive for being common, and her answer would read identically if every city in California were at the same rate.

ReasoningThis is a non sequitur: the conclusion does not follow from the premise, because prevailing practice and distributional burden are different questions. Answering an objection with an unrelated comparison retires it without engaging it. The objection remains open, and what would answer it is what the increase costs a low-income Berkeley household and what, if anything, was done to offset it.
Sources: California Department of Tax and Fee Administration, city sales and use tax rates effective July 1, 2026; Tax Policy Center, Who bears the burden of a national retail sales tax? See in transcript →
Adena Ishii02:17
“And it’s supported by the entire City Council, Berkeley Firefighters Local 1227, Alameda Labor Council, AFSCME Council 57, Building Trades Council, League of Women Voters, our City Auditor, Jenny Wong, and former City Auditor, Ann-Marie Hogan, and Alameda County Treasurer, Hank Levy.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Partly unverifiable

The Council vote checks out: passed to print June 16, 2026 by 9–0, none absent. The League of Women Voters, City Auditor Jenny Wong, County Treasurer Henry Levy and the Berkeley firefighters appear on the signed ballot arguments. Four names do not, and could not be confirmed from any public source — the Alameda Labor Council, AFSCME Council 57, the Building Trades Council, and former City Auditor Ann-Marie Hogan. The campaign site the ballot argument names for checking is password-protected.

Two of the endorsements that can be verified sit against their endorsers’ own published positions. Wong signed an argument attributing Berkeley’s position to “years of State takeaways” and “recent cuts by the Federal Government”; her office’s report Berkeley’s Financial Condition (FY2016–FY2025) finds a structural deficit — $32 million in FY2027, $33 million in FY2028 — on a ten-year record beginning well before those federal actions, and recommends recurring measures rather than one-time fixes. Hogan commissioned the 2013 Goldman School study Berkeley Based Budgeting and transmitted it to Council herself, seeking alternatives to the City’s “modified ‘across the board’ approach to General Fund budgeting”; it found the City Manager adjusts departmental funding by “criteria that do not appear in the Budget Documents, and are essentially invisible to the public,” and concluded across-the-board cuts are “ill equipped to address major structural changes.” The budget Measure V is asked to backfill was built by across-the-board reductions.

Neither endorsement is inconsistent on its face — a structural deficit can require revenue and prioritization both. But the reader who defers to the auditors has no way to know which of their two positions they are deferring to.

ReasoningThe list is fair evidence about whom voters trust and no evidence at all that the measure is sound: expert endorsement offered as proof is an appeal to authority, breadth of agreement offered as proof an appeal to popularity. The auditors show why the shortcut fails. Deferring to an expert only works if the expert’s judgment travels with the name, and here the same offices have taken a documented position that this argument contradicts — so the name arrives without the reasoning that made it worth trusting. The unanimity fails the same way: this Council votes unanimously as a matter of routine, so 9–0 on a measure it placed on the ballot itself is the expected result, not corroboration. What a voter would need is the fiscal case, which the roster is standing in for.
Sources: Ordinance No. 8,028-N.S., vote of June 16, 2026; Argument in Favor and Rebuttal to Argument Against Measure V; City Auditor, Berkeley’s Financial Condition (FY2016–FY2025); Berkeley Based Budgeting: Recommendations for Implementing Priority-Based Budgeting in the City of Berkeley, Goldman School of Public Policy, May 29, 2013, transmitted to Council by City Auditor Ann-Marie Hogan, April 1, 2014. Argument in Favor of Measure Vofficial ballot pamphlet
“Nothing is more important than the safety of our families and neighborhoods. … Your Yes on V vote would prevent forced cuts on Fire and Police Departments … prevent the closure of Fire Station 4, and maintain the 911 emergency response and neighborhood safety patrols we rely upon.”
— Argument in Favor of Measure V, signed by Mayor Adena Ishii and four others
Misleading

A Yes vote does not prevent any of it. Measure V is a general tax: the City Attorney’s impartial analysis states the revenue “could be spent on any valid municipal purpose,” and the ordinance’s only provision touching proceeds directs them to the General Fund and subjects them to the annual audit. Nothing earmarks a dollar for fire, police or 911, and nothing obliges a future Council to keep Station 4 open. The analysis also identifies no consequence, mandated cut or contingency if the measure fails — the closure is a budget choice on both branches of the outcome.

The premise sits against the budget it defends. If nothing is more important than public safety, a budget expressing that ranking would not have placed a fire station and nine positions on the reduction list; Berkeley reached the FY2027–28 plan through across-the-board departmental cuts, which is a method that declines to rank. Asked at the debate why fire was on the chopping block if it is the most important thing, Ishii did not answer — the exchange moved to closing statements.

ReasoningTwo claims are doing different work here. “Nothing is more important than public safety” is a statement of priority; “your Yes vote would prevent the closure of Fire Station 4” is a claim about what the measure does. The first is contradicted by a budget that ranked nothing, and the second by a general tax that commits nothing. Naming the service most likely to alarm a voter, while the instrument on the ballot cannot protect it, moves the decision away from the fiscal case and onto the fear.
Sources: Argument in Favor of Measure V; City Attorney’s Impartial Analysis of Measure V; Ordinance No. 8,028-N.S. §7.34.140; FY2027–FY2028 budget adoption record. See in transcript →
Argument in Favor of Measure Vofficial ballot pamphlet
“Learn more, visit safevibrantberkeley.com”
— Argument in Favor of Measure V, signed by Mayor Adena Ishii and four others
Needs context

The site is password-protected. Every request to safevibrantberkeley.com on September 4, 2026 returned HTTP 401 Unauthorized behind a Squarespace password gate, and the Internet Archive holds no capture of the domain at any date. The one place the official ballot argument sends voters for more information has not been open to them.

The gap matters because the argument is where Measure V’s purposes are named. The ordinance commits the revenue to the General Fund for any valid municipal purpose; the specific promises — Fire Station 4, 911 response, “vital youth services” — appear only in the argument and at the address it gives.

ReasoningA campaign is free to launch a website when it chooses. But the reference is printed in the Voter Information Pamphlet, which reaches every registered voter, and it is the argument’s only offer of substantiation. A voter who follows it finds a login prompt. Nothing here establishes intent; what it establishes is that the citation could not be checked on the date it was checked.
Sources: Argument in Favor of Measure V; HTTP response from safevibrantberkeley.com and www.safevibrantberkeley.com, September 4, 2026; Internet Archive Wayback Machine availability query, same date.
Eric Friedman03:11
“First, blaming Washington and Sacramento doesn’t fit the timeline. The auditor calls Berkeley’s deficit longstanding. Council said it knew the problem would recur, and the City’s own bond disclosure to investors said federal impacts were still uncertain. The problem was already here.”
— Eric Friedman
Supported

The core chronology is supported. Berkeley had identified a recurring structural imbalance before the latest federal-policy changes, and contemporaneous bond disclosure said the City could not yet predict the full effect of federal changes. The statement does not mean federal or state actions are irrelevant to Berkeley’s finances; it means they do not explain the pre-existing structural problem.

Eric Friedman04:28
“Twice in May and in June, the manager gave us the answer: ‘No alternatives were considered.’”
— Eric Friedman
Needs context

The June 23 staff report does state, under “Alternative Actions Considered”: “No alternative actions have been considered.” But that phrase should not be inflated into a claim that nobody inside City government ever discussed another idea. Employees submitted alternatives, and at least one option — mandatory time off — was modeled. The more precise criticism is that the adopted-budget record did not present a systematic alternatives analysis showing which major options were evaluated, accepted or rejected, and why.

Adena Ishii06:57
“I think this audience really understands the impacts of Prop 13. I myself fought very strong for Prop 13 reform because we know that that has completely impacted the City’s ability to raise the property taxes for corporations that have these massive properties throughout our cities around the State of California, preventing us from being able to have enough revenue that we need.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii, asked what alternative revenue measures Berkeley considered
Needs context

Proposition 13 has constrained California property taxation since 1978, and Ishii did participate in the League of Women Voters’ Prop 13 reform work. But that does not answer the moderator’s immediate question: what alternative revenue measures Berkeley considered instead of a sales tax in 2026. Nor does a decades-old constraint explain why the City’s longstanding structural deficit reached its current scale now.

ReasoningProp 13 is relevant background to California municipal finance. It becomes a distraction when invoked as though it establishes that Measure V is the appropriate present remedy, or demonstrates that other options were exhausted. Ishii’s personal advocacy also says nothing about whether that advocacy changed Berkeley’s fiscal capacity.
Sources: League of Women Voters Berkeley Albany Emeryville, board and Prop 13 Reform Team materials; California Legislative Analyst’s Office on Proposition 13. See in transcript →
Adena Ishii07:19
“We’ve had to make massive cuts. … We have made devastating cuts to the City already.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Needs context

The City made real reductions, but “massive” and “devastating” are characterizations rather than defined budget measures. Berkeley’s plan quantified General Fund reductions at about $29.3 million, or 10.3 percent of departmental and Charter Office expenditures, before positions and services were allocated to the proposed sales tax, and about $19.8 million, or 7.0 percent, after that allocation. The FY2027 Annual Appropriations Ordinance was about $922.3 million gross and $796.6 million net across all City funds.

ReasoningThe reductions should be described with a denominator. The citywide budget is not the correct denominator for a General Fund-only cut, but neither should readers be left with an adjective when the City has supplied the numbers.
Sources: FY2027–FY2028 proposed budget presentation; FY2027 Annual Appropriations Ordinance; 2026-06-23 Revised Agenda Packet. See in transcript →
Adena Ishii07:26
“What the opposition does not maybe understand is how painful it was to cut a number of different employees — over 100, I think nearly 180 employees that we had to cut in the City of Berkeley.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Misleading

The roughly 183 figure referred principally to positions, not 183 people being laid off. A large majority of the affected positions were vacant. At the relevant stage, City records described about 20 filled positions on the layoff list, with placement into vacancies expected to reduce actual layoffs to roughly 14.

ReasoningEliminating a vacant authorized position can reduce future service capacity and produce real workload consequences. But a vacant position is not an employee. Saying Berkeley “cut nearly 180 employees” materially overstates the number of people losing jobs.
Sources: May 19, June 16 and June 23, 2026 budget and Council records. See in transcript →
Adena Ishii07:42
“This is the right option. We’ve absolutely looked at it and other options as well.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Unsupported by the public record

The reviewed record establishes that other ideas existed. What it does not provide is a public, systematic analysis showing which major options were evaluated, how much each would save or raise, why high-value employee proposals were rejected or omitted, and why the half-cent sales tax emerged as the best remaining choice. The June 23 adopted-budget report itself said “No alternative actions have been considered.”

ReasoningThe assertion may describe internal conversations. Without the underlying analysis, it does not show the work necessary to support the conclusion that the sales tax was “the right option.”
Sources: 2026-06-23 Revised Agenda Packet; Measure V Public Records Act release; Employee Budget Survey Assertion Register. See in transcript →
Adena Ishii08:35
“This $10 million would close the current deficit gap that we’re looking at.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Needs context

This is defensible only if “current deficit gap” means the roughly $9.6 million FY2028 election-contingent gap remaining after other cuts and one-time measures. Adena Ishii09:36
“Anyone has access to any of the information that we have in terms of our budget.”
— Mayor Adena Ishii
Misleading

Core budget documents are public. But the statement is too broad. Material underlying information used in this fact check — including employee budget proposals and internal records concerning alternatives — had to be obtained through California Public Records Act requests. Eric Friedman09:46
“The revenue that we would see from the sales tax is somewhere between nine and ten million dollars. And so the gap is 30 and it’s growing. … The City expenses can outgrow the consumption tax that that represents. So over time, this gap will reappear in a few years. We’ll be back here having the same conversation.”
— Eric Friedman
Analysis

The structural mechanism is sound: adding a recurring revenue stream does not guarantee structural balance if recurring expenditures continue growing faster than recurring revenues. Berkeley’s own fiscal history documents that mismatch. The exact prediction that the gap will reappear “in a few years” is a forecast and cannot yet be rated as fact.

ReasoningThis is the long-term-stability argument the moderator asked about. Measure V can close the smaller election-contingent gap without solving the underlying growth mismatch that produced the larger structural problem.
Sources: City Auditor, Berkeley’s Financial Condition; 2026-06-23 Revised Agenda Packet; May–June 2026 Council budget records. See in transcript →
Eric Friedman10:16
“Doing things like a ten to twelve percent cut across the board isn’t a prioritization process. That’s arithmetic. You say everything is equally important and then you cut ten to twelve percent.”
— Eric Friedman
Analysis

This is a governance judgment, but the distinction is real. A uniform departmental reduction target is mathematically different from ranking services and programs by importance, effectiveness, cost-effectiveness or appropriate provider. The City made meaningful cuts; the question is whether the method constituted prioritization.

Eric Friedman11:52
“In December 2025, the City did show us their work. They looked at an equivalently valued home of equivalent size in Albany and in Oakland. And they told us that the property tax bill in Berkeley is $900 more than in Albany, and it’s $1,500 more than in Oakland. … You cannot bundle the benefits and unbundle the cost.”
— Eric Friedman
Supported

The document exists and the figures are close. Staff’s December 2, 2025 report to Council, prepared for this ballot discussion, priced an identical single-family residence in all three cities — $550,000 assessed value, 1,900 square feet, same homeowner’s exemption. Total current annual taxes: $10,924 in Berkeley, $10,008 in Albany, $9,365 in Oakland. That is $916 more than Albany and $1,559 more than Oakland, against the $900 and $1,500 stated from memory at the debate.

The comparison bears directly on the parity argument. Berkeley’s higher bill is driven by its special taxes and school taxes rather than the base rate: $2,013 in city special taxes against Oakland’s $1,397 and Albany’s $491, and $1,504 in school district special taxes against Oakland’s $435 and Albany’s $591. Berkeley’s library tax alone is $680, against Oakland’s $319 and Albany’s $285.

ReasoningParity in one tax is not parity in the tax bill. A household pays from one wallet, and on the City’s own figures a Berkeley household already pays roughly $900 to $1,600 more per year than the same household in the cities offered as the benchmark — before this half cent, and before the other Berkeley measures on the same ballot. The comparison is Berkeley’s own, prepared by staff for the discussion that produced these measures.
Sources: City of Berkeley, Discussion Regarding Potential Ballot Measures for the November 3, 2026, General Municipal Election, December 2, 2025 — Summary of FY 2026 Property-Based Taxes and Assessments Comparison. See in transcript →

Needs context

The employee survey produced 59 responses. Forty-nine contained at least one cost-saving idea and 35 contained at least one revenue idea. Calling an opponent’s statements “misinformation” does not establish that they are false. Ishii did immediately identify two factual disputes — whether employee suggestions were taken into account, and whether the sales tax would close the deficit — and those claims are checked on their merits above and below.

The support of unions, elected officials and auditors may be relevant to voters deciding whom they trust. It is not evidence that the contested fiscal claims are correct.

What the checks show

The debate contains several genuine agreements that should not be obscured. Berkeley has a structural fiscal problem. The City made real budget reductions. Measure V would raise real recurring General Fund revenue. The dispute is about what those facts prove.

The records reviewed here support five narrower conclusions.

  • The structural deficit predates the recent federal and state funding pressures invoked in the debate. Those pressures can make the problem worse; they did not create it.
  • The “nearly 180 employees” description confuses positions with people. Most of the affected positions were vacant, and expected actual layoffs were far smaller.
  • Measure V can cover the smaller ballot-contingent gap; it does not itself erase a roughly $30 million structural imbalance. The debate repeatedly moved between those two quantities.
  • The City did not publicly demonstrate a comprehensive alternatives process. Employee ideas and some internal modeling existed, but the published record did not show a systematic disposition of the major alternatives or explain why the sales tax was superior.
  • Endorsements are endorsements, not fiscal analysis. The repeated invocation of Council, unions, auditors and civic organizations cannot establish the tax’s necessity or answer the objections about regressivity, prioritization and long-term structural balance.

The strongest question left by the exchange is the one Friedman posed in his opening: what discipline will keep spending from outrunning the new revenue too?
