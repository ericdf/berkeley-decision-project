Berkeley Decision Project

Better questions. Better evidence. Better choices.

Berkeley makes consequential public decisions without a consistent method for defining problems, comparing alternatives, understanding costs, and measuring results. This project proposes one.

Public decisions improve when we define the problem before choosing the solution, consider the full range of alternatives, compare costs and consequences over the whole life of a commitment, and measure what actually happens afterward.

How should we make decisions?

Start with the role of government

Before asking how to pay for something, ask whether the City must ensure it happens, should pursue it with partners, or should decline, transfer, repair or stop it.

The goal is not less government. It is government capable of doing important things well.

And yet Berkeley repeatedly makes consequential decisions without doing it.

Where does Berkeley go wrong?

These are recurring patterns in Berkeley's own record — not isolated episodes, and not problems that disappear when the officeholders change.

The Crayon Box

Berkeley routinely considers ways to raise more money while leaving ways to change what it spends in the box.

Checkpoints That Don’t Check

Conditions meant to stop or reconsider projects fail to trigger when the conditions arrive.

Bonds Win Elections; Budget Discipline Does Not

Raising new money creates a visible accomplishment; reallocating existing money does not.

Scrutiny Follows Controversy

Council scrutiny tracks present controversy better than the size or duration of the commitment.

Berkeley’s Hidden Budget Cycle

A second budget cycle appropriates hundreds of millions each year. Council adopts it without discussion.

Which Version, Never Whether

Public engagement often asks residents which option they prefer without offering “don’t do it” as an option.

You Can’t Prioritize What You Can’t Measure

Berkeley measures activity better than outcomes, leaving Council little basis for ranking programs.

If You Build It, a Plan Will Come

Capital projects can advance without first establishing how their ongoing operations will be funded.

Buy Once, Pay Forever

Decisions emphasize acquisition cost while pushing maintenance, subsidies and other lifecycle costs into the future.

Governance by Exhaustion

Council meetings routinely stretch late into the night as elected officials hear hours of repetitive testimony, sometimes on matters over which the City has little or no authority.

About this project. Built from Berkeley's primary record: meeting transcripts, agendas, votes, audits, budgets and audited financial statements. Figures are traceable to their sources; unsupported ones are removed.

Berkeley Decision Project and Council Scorecards are related civic projects. They share research and a commitment to documented evidence, but serve different purposes: this project develops better approaches to public decisions; Council Scorecards evaluates elected officials.
