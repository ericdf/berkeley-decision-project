Start with the role of government

A framework for deciding what government should do

Before asking how to pay for something, ask whether the City must ensure it happens, should pursue it with partners, or should decline, transfer, repair or stop it. That question comes first, and it is answerable.

The goal is not less government. It is government capable of doing important things well.

Recurring decision problems

Institutional habits visible in Berkeley's own record

These are patterns, not episodes. Each is documented from primary sources — budgets, audits, staff reports, agendas and meeting transcripts — and each would survive a complete change of personnel.

Not only because the rules permit them. Most of these habits persist in the presence of an available alternative that nobody reaches for: a recommendation that could have been amended and was not, an item that could have been pulled and was not, a question that could have been asked first and was asked afterward. What makes a habit durable is partly procedural and partly cultural — what a body treats as normal, what it considers worth the friction of objecting, and what it has come to expect of itself. Rules can be rewritten in an afternoon. Expectations are what survive an election.

The Crayon Box

There are at least seven ways to close a deficit. Berkeley reaches for four, and every one of them raises money from somebody. The three that would change what the city spends stay in the box.

Checkpoints That Don’t Check

A condition written to stop a project unless funding appears does not stop it. Reviews get routed past the body with jurisdiction. The document holding the decisive numbers is never presented to the people voting the money. Years and millions later, nobody has decided whether the city can — or should — proceed.

Bonds Win Elections; Budget Discipline Does Not

A chronically underfunded service has two fixes: raise new money, or move money from something else. Only one produces a highly visible political accomplishment. The other leaves no trace a voter could ever be shown.

Scrutiny Follows Controversy

One calendar gets debated; the other is adopted in a single motion. Which pile an item lands in has little relationship to what it commits the city to. The record suggests scrutiny follows present controversy instead: contested items get discussion; accumulating obligations often do not.

Which Version, Never Whether

A survey can only measure the choices it offers. Berkeley asks residents which design, which location, which of these priorities — rarely whether to proceed at all. The result is a percentage that measures agreement with a prepared list, and then travels as though it measured public support for the decision.

You Can’t Prioritize What You Can’t Measure

Berkeley mostly counts activity rather than outcomes. Without knowing what programs accomplish for what they cost, Council cannot reliably rank them — making across-the-board cuts less a priority decision than a substitute for one.

If You Build It, a Plan Will Come

A capital project gets appropriated, designed and built. What it costs to run is decided later, by different people, out of a different budget. Nothing in the sequence requires that number before the doors open — so the question arrives when the building does, and by then the answer changes nothing.

Buy Once, Pay Forever

Berkeley compares what commitments cost to start, but rarely what they cost to carry. Maintenance, reserves, follow-on subsidies and revenue effects sit outside the comparison that decides the purchase. The City’s own records show those costs arriving later, one appropriation at a time.

Decision tools

Make the tradeoffs visible for yourself
⚙ Interactive — try the Crayon Box yourself

The Crayon Box argues that Berkeley reaches for the four options that raise money and doesn’t touch the three that would change what it spends. The Berkeley Budget Lab puts you in the chair with the City Manager’s own scenarios: protect a department, accept its cut, or make it absorb more. Every service you protect pushes the savings onto one you did not — which is the choice reprioritizing forces, and the reason it is rarely proposed.

⚙ Interactive — what the habits cost a household

These habits arrive as a bill. The Berkeley Tax Explorer lets you do that calculation comprehensively — every 2026 measure together with what you already pay, modeled against your own household rather than a typical one, and projected out thirty years. It runs entirely in your browser: no account, no cookies, no server, nothing collected, nothing shared.

Measure FF reached voters as a rate — 17 cents per square foot of residential improvements — next to the aggregate it would raise, about $15 million a year. Both true; neither is what a household pays. The voter had to do the multiplication: 17 cents is $255 a year on a 1,500-square-foot home, $340 on 2,000, $425 on 2,500. A rate looks small, an aggregate looks like someone else’s money, and the number in between is the one nobody prints.

And nobody rolls them up. Each measure seemed modest in isolation. The butcher’s bill is the cumulative tax burden — and assembling it is left to you, which is why new revenue is the easy crayon.

About this project. Berkeley Decision Project is built from the primary record: meeting transcripts, annotated agendas, linked votes, audits, budgets and the City's audited financial statements. Every figure is traceable to a document, and where a figure could not be reproduced from the source it is attributed to, it has been dropped and the omission stated on the page. The project is maintained by a Berkeley resident, not a journalist or an officeholder.

Berkeley Decision Project and Council Scorecards are related civic projects. They share research and a commitment to documented evidence, but serve different purposes: this project develops better approaches to public decisions; Council Scorecards evaluates elected officials.
