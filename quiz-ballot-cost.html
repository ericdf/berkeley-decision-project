What Will This Ballot Cost?

The November measures do not arrive on a blank tax bill. This quiz works out what the ballot would cost three modeled Berkeley households — and what is already changing on their bill regardless.

Question 1 of 6

Mary's 1,500-square-foot home has an assessed value of $1,000,000. The City estimates that the $300 million general-obligation bond (Measure U) will initially cost about $22.14 per $100,000 of assessed value. About what does that mean for Mary for a year?

At $22.14 for each $100,000 of assessed value, a $1 million assessment produces an estimated annual cost of $221.40.

Across the three modeled households:

  • Joan and Steve — assessed $500,000: about $110.70 a year
  • Mary — assessed $1,000,000: about $221.40 a year
  • Bill and Ted — assessed $1,500,000: about $332.10 a year
In fairnessThis is the City's estimate, not a guaranteed annual payment for the life of the bonds. What a household actually pays varies with when and how much the City issues, interest rates at issuance, and changes in assessed value — the subject of the next question.

Question 2 of 6

That roughly $221.40 is an estimate, not a promise that Mary's bill will stay there. Which of these could change what Mary pays for the $300 million general-obligation bond (Measure U) in future years?

Select all that apply.
That roughly $221.40 is an estimate, not a promise that Mary's bill will stay there. Which of these could change what Mary pays for the $300 million general-obligation bond (Measure U) in future years? Select all that apply.

Mary's own tax base can change. Her assessed value is not necessarily frozen. Under California's Proposition 13 framework, assessed value can generally rise by up to 2% annually absent other reassessment events.

The financing can change. Interest rates and the timing and amount of individual bond issuances affect how much debt service must be collected.

The citywide tax base can change. Changes in Berkeley's total assessed value affect how the required debt service is spread across taxable property.

The other three are real costs, but they do not drive this levy. Mary's shopping affects sales taxes, not a property-tax debt service. Parking rates are unrelated. Construction costs affect what the bond money can buy rather than what Mary is charged — a question this series returns to later.

Question 3 of 6

Joan and Steve, Mary, and Bill and Ted all live in 1,500-square-foot homes. Their assessed values range from $500,000 to $1,500,000. Under the proposed public-bank parcel tax, which is based on square footage, who pays the most?

At the working rate of six cents per square foot:

1,500 sq ft × $0.06 = $90 a year.

All three pay the same in this modeled example, because the tax follows square footage rather than assessed value:

  • Joan and Steve — assessed $500,000, 1,500 sq ft: $90
  • Mary — assessed $1,000,000, 1,500 sq ft: $90
  • Bill and Ted — assessed $1,500,000, 1,500 sq ft: $90

Different measures on the same ballot use different tax bases. That is what makes the total hard to add up.

In fairnessThe public-bank measure reached the ballot by initiative petition. Council submitted it to the voters without alteration on July 14, 2026 rather than placing it.

Question 4 of 6

Mary buys a $1,500 laptop. The ballot contains two separate proposed sales-tax increases: Berkeley's half-cent City sales tax (Measure V), and the regional transit sales-tax measure. Assuming each adds one-half percentage point, what happens if both pass?

The two half-cent increases apply to the same taxable purchase. Together they add 1 percentage point to the sales-tax rate.

On a $1,500 laptop:

$1,500 × 1% = $15 more sales tax.

Neither measure replaces the other, and Mary does not pay only the larger of the two. They stack on the same purchase.

In fairnessOnly one of these is a Berkeley City Council measure. The regional transit tax was placed on the ballot by a regional body, not by Berkeley.

Question 5 of 6

Suppose a Berkeley household makes about $2,000 a month in purchases subject to sales tax. If both half-cent measures pass, about how much additional sales tax would that household pay over a year?

$2,000 × 12 months = $24,000 in annual taxable purchases.

An additional combined 1% = $240 a year, or about $20 a month.

For scale: at about $29 a pizza, $240 is roughly eight Cheese Board pizzas.

In fairnessThe $2,000-a-month figure is a modeled example, not a claim about what an average Berkeley household spends. The pizza comparison is there to give the number an intuitive scale, not to suggest a better use of the money.
Question 6 of 6

On June 30, 2026, the Berkeley City Council authorized the third and final $20 million tranche of bonds from the $100 million Measure T1 infrastructure bond that voters approved in 2016. That issuance raises the T1 debt-service rate from 0.0110% to 0.0140% — an increase of 0.0030 percentage points. Bill and Ted's home is assessed at $1,500,000. Even if Measure U fails in November, about how much additional annual T1 tax appears on their bill?

$1,500,000 × 0.0030% = $45 a year.

  • Joan and Steve — assessed $500,000: $15 a year
  • Mary — assessed $1,000,000: $30 a year
  • Bill and Ted — assessed $1,500,000: $45 a year

This is not one of the November measures. It comes from the already-approved 2016 Measure T1 bond, and it arrives regardless of how any November measure is decided.

The existing tax baseline is itself moving while voters consider new taxes.
