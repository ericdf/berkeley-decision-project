Berkeley Decision Project

Did Berkeley’s City Council vibe its way to a $300 million bond?

A step-by-step reconstruction of the decisions that produced Measure U, from the first staff recommendation to the ballot. Each step is taken from the City’s own record.

Measure U · decision process · October 2026

What happened

  1. The starting point
    Observed
    Infrastructure needs of more than $1.5 billion

    The City estimates its long-term infrastructure needs exceed $1.5 billion. Any bond will cover a fraction of that, which makes choosing what to fund the central decision.

    March 17, 2026 staff report, p. 3

    Berkeley’s backlog is far larger than any bond. That makes the first question which work to fund, and how much of it.

  2. December 2, 2025
    Observed
    Staff propose a GO bond of “up to $300 million”

    The first recommendation asks Council to “gauge voter interest for a General Obligation (GO) bond on the November 2026 ballot that could generate up to $300 million.” No survey had been run and no project list existed.

    The figure came with a tax-rate case: combined with existing bonds, an average rate of $44 per $100,000 of assessed value, “less than the historical average tax rate.”

    December 2, 2025 work session, Special Item 2 staff report, pp. 2, 5

    The amount arrives first, in the first sentence of the first recommendation, justified by what the tax rate can bear.

  3. December 2, 2025

    Councilmember Blackaby reads the tax-rate chart as headroom.

  4. February 19–23, 2026
    Observed
    Survey tests $200 million against $300 million

    Lake Research Partners polled 500 likely voters. Half heard a $300 million bond, half a $200 million bond.

    71%yes on $300M
    70%yes on $200M
    72% / 63%$300M / $200M after pro and con arguments
    March 17, 2026 staff report, pp. 2, 19 (survey presentation)

    The larger bond polls no worse than the smaller one, and holds up better under argument.

  5. March 17, 2026 · the pollster’s reading

    The pollster’s conclusion: a larger number that costs no support is a number to put on the ballot.

  6. March 17, 2026 · 00:47:45

    Councilmember O’Keefe takes the survey result to its next step.

  7. March 17, 2026 · 00:49

    Asked where $300 million came from, staff answer with how it feels and what it costs per $100,000.

  8. March 17 → June 16, 2026
    Observed
    $300 MILLION

    Council unanimously directs ballot language “for a potential $300 million General Obligation bond measure,” and on June 16 places it on the ballot. Measure U’s text: “The estimated cost of the Improvements to be funded by the Bonds is $300,000,000.”

    March 17, 2026 staff report, p. 1; annotated agenda, March 17, 2026; Measure U full text, §3

    The amount proposed in December is the amount on the ballot.

STOP.

Before deciding how much Berkeley should borrow, what had the City established it should buy and can deliver?

The questions that should set the amount

  1. Decision gate 1 · input
    What outcomes have the highest public value?

    A large backlog does not make every capital expenditure equally valuable. The first task is to identify the outcomes worth buying.

    On the record: the City’s Parks, Recreation and Waterfront Commission told Council the staff report shows “no scoring, no weighting, no documented analysis,” and that the director confirmed “there was no formal scoring system” (April 14, 2026 report, p. 5).

    First, decide what is worth buying.

  2. Decision gate 2 · input
    Which of those outcomes can Berkeley actually deliver?

    Delivery capacity belongs in the investment decision. A desirable project the City cannot reliably execute is not a deliverable outcome.

    Then, what the City can actually build.

  3. Decision gate 3 · input
    Which outcomes will Berkeley commit to delivering?

    Example projects and broad eligible categories are not commitments. A commitment is what survives the vote.

    Then, what it will commit to.

  4. Decision gate 4 · input
    Are the benefits durable enough to justify the expense and duration of the debt?

    Long-lived borrowing should buy benefits that last about as long as the burden it places on future taxpayers.

    Then, whether the benefits outlast the debt.

  5. Decision gate 5 · input
    What will those committed outcomes cost?

    Price the defined portfolio after deciding what is valuable, deliverable and worth committing to.

    On the record: Berkeley’s March 17 presentation scored candidate projects “within a $100M per-category cap ($300M total)” (March 17, 2026 staff report, p. 81).

    Then, what that portfolio costs.

  6. Output
    How much should Berkeley borrow?

    The bond amount should be the output of this process, not an input to it.

    Only then, the amount.

The bond amount should be the output of this process, not an input to it.

Two sequences

What happened

  1. Infrastructure needs
  2. Propose “up to $300 million” at a tolerable tax rate
  3. Test what voters might tolerate: $200M vs. $300M
  4. “No reason to not put the higher $300m amount on the ballot”
  5. “How far can we take this?”
  6. $300 million
  7. Broad authorization

What should determine the bond

  1. Infrastructure needs
  2. Identify highest-value outcomes
  3. Test delivery capacity
  4. Choose outcomes Berkeley will commit to
  5. Match benefit durability to debt
  6. Price the committed outcomes
  7. Determine required borrowing

The process discovered how much voters might tolerate before establishing what Berkeley should buy and can deliver.

The oversight comes after the decision

Measure U promises accountability: annual reports, commission oversight and audits. All of it sits downstream of the amount.

  1. $300 million

    The amount, fixed first.

  2. Observed
    Broad authorization

    The improvements “include, but are not limited to” seven categories, from fire facilities to “other City infrastructure.”

    Measure U full text, §2, p. 3

    Spent on categories, not a list.

  3. Observed
    Reports and oversight

    An annual status report from the City Manager; annual reports and recommendations from two commissions.

    Measure U full text, §6(E)–(F), p. 4

    Reports arrive every year.

  4. Observed
    Audit

    The City Auditor reviews expenditures at least once every three years “to confirm that Bond expenditures are consistent with the intent of this Measure.”

    Measure U full text, §6(G), p. 5

    An audit every three years.

But what is the audit measuring against?

  • Binding project commitments?NOImprovements “include, but are not limited to” categories (§2).
  • Required quantities?NOThe measure names no quantity of anything to be built or repaired.
  • Delivery deadlines?NOImprovements “will be completed as needed” (§2).
  • Guaranteed completion?NO“The City Council cannot guarantee that the Bonds will provide sufficient funds to allow completion of all needed Improvements” (§2).
  • Binding delivery outcomes?NOVoters approve purposes in broad categories (§2); the measure specifies no delivery outcome against which to hold the City.

The reports the measure requires are information delivered to Council. None obliges Council to decide anything when a project slips, shrinks or disappears. That is reporting, not an enforceable delivery guarantee.

The audit can ask

Was the money spent consistently with Measure U’s authorized purposes?

The audit cannot enforce a promise that doesn’t exist

Measure U does not give the Auditor a binding baseline of particular projects, quantities, deadlines, or guaranteed outcomes against which to measure delivery.

A robust process for auditing spending is not a substitute for a robust process for deciding what taxpayers are buying.

Define the outcomes first. Establish that Berkeley can deliver them. Commit to them. Price them. Then determine how much to borrow.

Start with what is worth delivering. Commit to it. Price it. Then decide how much to borrow.
