Fungibility Isn’t Fun

Berkeley voters had every reason to understand Measure FF as an addition to the money already going to streets and sidewalks. What happened instead shows why “new revenue” and “new spending” are not the same thing.

How a dedicated tax can produce less new spending than its headline · September 2026

Berkeley’s streets and sidewalks were already visibly neglected. Measure T1 had money programmed for paving, and the City had other existing street funding. Then Measure FF came along promising roughly $15 million a year for streets, sidewalks, safety, and related improvements.

A voter could reasonably have pulled the lever with something close to relief: at last, the meager existing investment would be joined by a substantial new stream of money.

Or would it?

Within a year, Berkeley removed the remaining $3.95 million of Measure T1 street funding because, in staff’s words, “other funding was identified.”

The other funding was Measure FF.

“Other funding was identified so existing T1 funds could be reallocated.”

— City staff, September 2025

The new FF money stayed dedicated to its promised purposes. But some of the money that had already been headed to streets did not stay there.

That is the central problem.

When voters approve a new tax for something the City already funds, they naturally want to know how much more that service will receive. A $15 million tax sounds like $15 million of additional investment. But if the arrival of the new money frees old money to be spent elsewhere, the net increase can be smaller than the headline number.

Berkeley even tried to prevent this. Measure FF said its tax would be available only “provided other funding for street maintenance is maintained,” and it included a formal maintenance-of-effort requirement.

The safeguard still left a hole large enough for the remaining T1 street allocation to pass through.

Before FF, the City counted both

Measure T1 was Berkeley’s 2016 $100 million infrastructure bond. Its later Phase 2 project process programmed about $6.75 million for streets.

Council reduced that allocation by $2.8 million in 2023, leaving roughly $3.95 million.

Then voters approved Measure FF in November 2024.

One month later, a City audit-status report was still counting both sources for FY2025–26:

$3.95 million of remaining T1 street money
plus
the newly approved Measure FF revenue

That is the additive picture a voter would naturally expect.

Nine months later, the T1 contribution was gone.

December 10, 2024 audit-status report

Dedicated does not automatically mean additional

Suppose a program has:

$15.3M recurring funding
+ $3.95M supplemental funding
= $19.25M

Then voters approve a new $15M tax.

If all the old money stays:

$19.25M + $15M = $34.25M

If the $3.95M supplement is removed:

$15.3M + $15M = $30.3M

The new tax still generated $15 million.

But the net increase over the previous funding level is only $11.05 million.

That simplified example is not Berkeley’s exact annual paving budget. It shows the accounting problem.

A voter hears “$15 million in new revenue” and reasonably asks how much more the service will receive.

That answer depends on what happens to the money that was already there.

FF tried to prevent this

This is what makes the example more important.

FF anticipated the danger of supplanting. It included a maintenance-of-effort rule precisely so the City could not simply approve a new street tax and then slash its existing street budget.

But the rule protected a floor: at least $15.3 million of qualifying non-FF street-maintenance funding.

T1 sat above that floor as supplemental, one-time capital funding.

Berkeley’s own capital plan had described bond money such as T1 as funding that supplemented recurring street sources.

So the City could satisfy FF’s maintenance-of-effort test while removing the separate T1 contribution.

What FF protected

The recurring non-FF street-funding floor

What it left exposed

Supplemental street money already programmed above that floor

That is why the ballot phrase “provided other funding for street maintenance is maintained” deserves more scrutiny than it received.

The rule protected a defined baseline. It did not protect every dollar a voter could reasonably understand “other funding” to include.

An audit can miss this entirely

FF has real accountability machinery: dedicated accounts, reporting, oversight, and City Auditor review.

Those controls can establish that FF money itself went where the measure required.

But consider two different questions:

Did Berkeley spend every FF dollar on an eligible FF purpose?

and

How much more did Berkeley spend on those purposes after FF arrived?

The first is a fund-compliance question.

The second requires looking outside the FF fund to see what happened to every other source.

A perfectly clean FF audit could coexist with a large substitution elsewhere.

Earmarking controls the new dollar. Anti-supplanting controls the old dollar.

That distinction should matter every time Berkeley proposes a dedicated tax.

What voters should be shown

Every new revenue measure should come with a simple gross-to-net table:

QuestionWhat voters should see
What was already being spent?Recurring baseline from every source
What supplemental money was already committed?Bonds, grants, one-time appropriations, adopted capital allocations
What will the new measure generate?Gross new revenue
What old funding can disappear?Every source legally available for reduction or reallocation
What actually remains after substitutions?Net-new resources
Where does displaced money go?Destination of every material reallocation

That would have made the FF/T1 interaction visible before the T1 money moved.

A dedicated tax tells you where the new money must go.

It does not tell you whether the old money will stay there.
