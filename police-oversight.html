Police oversight

Berkeley’s Police Oversight Answers to the Council, Behind Closed Doors

Measure II made Berkeley’s police oversight office independent of the City Manager and gave it the power to demand police records. When it fought over records, over its rules and over its Director’s job, each fight ended with the City Council: the records fight because the office could not go to court without the Council’s permission, the other two in closed session. None of those outcomes came with public reasons.

In November 2020 Berkeley voters approved Measure II, which rewrote the City Charter to create a Police Accountability Board and an Office of the Director of Police Accountability. The Charter makes both “independent of the City Manager” (§125(4)(a)). It orders every City department to produce the records the Board or its staff request, “without redaction or limitation” unless state or federal law requires otherwise, within ten business days (§125(20)(a)), and it gives the Director and the Board the power to issue subpoenas (§125(20)(c)). Measure II full text

The Charter left three things with the Council. The Council funds the office “as the City Council determines resources allow” (§125(4)(b)). The Mayor and each Councilmember nominate one Board member, and the Council approves each nominee (§125(6)(a)). And the Council may remove the Director “by a two-thirds vote either on its own motion or based on the recommendation of the Police Accountability Board” (§125(14)(e)). The Board may recommend removal only “for cause” (§125(14)(d)). In 2025 and 2026 the Council used every one of those powers, and it made the decisions that mattered most out of public view.

The Charter made the office independent of the City Manager. It left the office’s records, rules and leadership with the Council.

Records: a right that needs the Council’s permission to enforce

In April 2025 the Board asked the Council to “pass an ordinance or otherwise affirm the Police Accountability Board’s ability to access documents and records.” The Council’s Public Safety Policy Committee had sent the request forward with a negative recommendation. The Council instead adopted Councilmember Blackaby’s resolution affirming support for the Police Department, the Board and the office, 8–1, with Councilmember Lunaparra voting no. No records-access ordinance followed. April 15, 2025 annotated agenda, Items 11a and 11b

That summer the office investigated a complaint about a June 2025 encampment sweep. It requested records in July and issued a subpoena on August 13, according to then-Director Hansel Aguilar’s later lawsuit. Police Chief Jennifer Louis replied in September that the records still withheld “are not relevant to this specific misconduct complaint investigation,” as Berkeleyside reported. On September 30, Aguilar told the Council: “There were some barriers to obtain this critical information and it was a difference of opinion on law.” He asked the Council to support a state law letting oversight agencies request help from the Attorney General directly. The Council voted to take no action, 8–0, with Councilmember Lunaparra abstaining. September 30, 2025 annotated agenda, Item 17 · transcript

In December Aguilar petitioned the Alameda County Superior Court to order the Chief to produce the records. The City Attorney’s Office, which the Charter names as the Board’s and the Director’s legal adviser (§125(15)(a)), opposed him. Its filing said the petition “should never have been filed” because Aguilar had not sought the Council’s permission, and that the Chief had turned over all records relevant to the claims against specific officers, as the Berkeley Scanner reported. In February, Berkeleyside reported, a judge denied the petition, finding that Aguilar had no authority to file it. On July 14, 2026, the Board’s chair told the Council that “we ultimately did not get those records.” July 14, 2026 transcript, 1:08:46

The Charter says departments “shall” produce records within ten business days. When the Police Department declined, the City’s position, and as reported the court’s, was that the office could not go to court to enforce that sentence without the Council’s permission.

Rules: a dispute settled in closed session

The Board approved permanent regulations for handling complaints against officers in April 2023, according to the resignation statement of its two longest-serving members, Kitty Calavita and Juliet Leftwich. Because the rules affect officers’ working conditions, the City must negotiate them with the police union, and before that the Board negotiated with an “internal stakeholders” group: the Chief and representatives of the City Manager’s, Human Resources and City Attorney’s offices. In December 2025, they wrote, that group “appeared before the City Council in closed session for Council to resolve the dispute,” and “Council overwhelmingly sided with the Chief and other internal stakeholders.” They resigned on January 30, 2026. The Board’s February 11 minutes list four members in its nine seats. Joint resignation statement · PAB minutes, February 11, 2026

On March 24, Mayor Ishii read into the record the statement of the newly appointed interim Director, Kathy Lee: “It’s more than a little sad to me that permanent regulations for handling complaints against officers are still not in place.” March 24, 2026 transcript, 3:48:15

The Director: removed with no reason given

The friction was public by September 30, 2025, when Aguilar brought two items straight to the full Council after declining the Agenda and Rules Committee’s request to send them through a policy committee. Mayor Ishii told him: “It just feels like you are in constant opposition with what we are recommending to you.” Aguilar answered: “I’ve been here three years. I’ve been consistently asking for a performance evaluations, to be publicly given a performance evaluation like this… I think we should have a closed session conversation if there’s other concerns about my performance.” The Mayor said consultants would evaluate all the Council’s charter officers. September 30, 2025 transcript

The Council’s December 1, 2025 closed-session agenda listed a performance evaluation of the Director. On February 9, 2026, it met in closed session on a “public employee discipline/dismissal/release” and an anticipated employment claim against the City. Berkeleyside and the Berkeley Scanner reported that eight members voted to remove Aguilar, with Councilmember Kesarwani absent. The City has posted no annotated agenda or written report of action for that meeting; the Mayor’s confirmation reached the public through the press. The Board’s chair, Joshua Cayetano, told Berkeleyside that “neither myself nor the Police Accountability Board were consulted by the City Council prior to his dismissal.” December 1 agenda · February 9 agenda

No Councilmember stated a reason for the removal in open session in any Council transcript in this project’s record, which begins in December 2024. The Board did not recommend removal for cause; the Council acted without one, as §125(14)(e) allows. Personnel law limits what officials may say in public about an employee, and Aguilar’s May 2026 lawsuit against the City and the Mayor makes claims no court has tested. Those limits account for silence about this case. They leave a standing arrangement: a Director whose job is to compel records from the Police Department can be removed by the Council at any time, by a vote taken in closed session, with no reason ever stated.

The next contract drops the evaluation

Aguilar’s 2022 contract required the Council, within three months of his appointment, to “jointly establish objective, verifiable measures” of his performance, and to evaluate him at the end of each year. October 11, 2022 item and draft contract

On September 29, 2026, the Council confirmed Mary Opler as Director on its consent calendar, all ayes, with no discussion. Her contract lets the Council end her employment “at any time, with or without cause,” and creates no right to “advance notice of termination, appeal, disciplinary hearing, progressive discipline, performance improvement plan, statement of reasons for termination.” It adds: “No performance evaluation or assessment shall be required, either before the City Council exercises its authority to terminate Employee’s employment or at any other time.” Severance falls from two months’ salary for each month of service, capped at twelve months, to a flat five months. September 29, 2026 annotated agenda, Item 8 · agenda packet, pp. 143–150

After removing a Director without a stated reason, the Council approved a contract under which the next Director has no right to a reason and need never be evaluated.

Seats and staff

The Council fills the Board. Its annotated agendas show an appointment on June 3, 2025, and none again until April 21, 2026. Between April and July 2026 the Council appointed five members and the Board’s first alternate. The City’s commission roster of October 19, 2026 lists seven members and the alternate, with the District 3 and District 8 seats vacant. Commission roster

The FY 2026 budget, adopted June 24, 2025 by a 5–3 vote with one abstention, paid for its additions in part by freezing the office’s analyst position ($172,848) and investigator position ($203,444) for the year. June 24, 2025 annotated agenda, Item 24

What a rule would look like

Each of these decisions was within the Council’s authority. What the record lacks is any requirement that the Council explain them. Four procedural rules would supply it:

  • Reasons before removal. When the Council moves to remove the Director without a Board recommendation, it gives the Director and the Board a written statement of reasons and hears the Board before it votes.
  • Published measures and an annual evaluation. Restore the 2022 contract’s terms, and let the Director choose to hold the evaluation in public.
  • A records path that does not run through the Council. When a department withholds records and the City Attorney represents the department, the Director may retain separate counsel under §125(15) without further authorization, or the dispute goes to a neutral hearing officer with a deadline.
  • Vacancies on the agenda. Each Board vacancy, and how long it has been open, is listed on the Council agenda until it is filled.

The sentence the Council would have had to say is ordinary: why, in public, before it acts.
